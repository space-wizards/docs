# Lore Pillars  
  
## Kitchen Sink  
Space Station is a pulpy kitchen sink setting and is by no means restricted to the conventions of any one genre. Time traveling demon clowns, cybernetic cowboys, space wizards - anything goes!  
  
## Direct and Unpretentious  
Space Station's storytelling should not beat around the bush. Our zombies are called zombies, our dragons are called dragons, our wizards use magic - basically, present things in the most straightforward and direct way possible. If your idea was borrowed from an existing work, name it after the original. If a concept does not neatly map onto any existing trope, try to give it a name that's direct and descriptive, and avoid using unfamiliar proper nouns for key parts of the game world unless absolutely necessary.  
  
## Ambiguous  
An important element of the Space Station setting is ambiguity. As a canvas for roleplay, the game should avoid dictating any details of the setting which aren't directly related to in-game mechanics or factions. Answers to questions like "Why is the Syndicate attacking the station?" or "What happened to Earth?" should remain unstated and left to players to invent as they play. Likewise, proper nouns like faction and place names should be used sparingly.  
  
As a subrule, Space Station does not generally have a lot of named "famous people" in its setting. While there is room for incidental celebrities or urban legends, there is no extensive roster of lore-critical figures like generals or presidents, and what few do exist should have biographies that can be summed up in at most a sentence or two ("CEO of Nanotrasen" or "Founder of the Space Clown Consortium" for example). Care should be taken not to make named characters *too* cool - the players should always be the main characters in our story, and named characters should be treated as Space Station's extras, placed into the world as, at most, set dressing.  
  
## Postmodern and Morally Relative  
The universe of Space Station is cruel, uncaring, and unknowable. Members of the crew should feel like cogs in the machine, interchangeable parts of an uncaring corporate apparatus. There are no epic battles between absolute good and true evil, and there should never be any central authority from which the crew can draw legitimacy, such as a police or military. What gods the setting has are capricious and usually absent, and even the crew's employers should feel distant and unreachable. If something goes wrong, there is no higher authority you can count on to save you.  
  
Relatedly, even though the station is constantly besieged by secret agents, paramilitary goons, and supernatural forces, life on the station should still retain an appropriate degree of mundanity. This is not a setting where everyone is special - even in a world full of killer cyborgs and space monsters, someone still has to mop the floors, after all. Conversely, even a grandmaster wizard or a supersoldier pumped to the gills with combat drugs is not immune to slipping on a banana peel and sliding face-first into a trash chute.
